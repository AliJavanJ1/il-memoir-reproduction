\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the theory predicted, and what the runs show}

\paragraph{$f$-PG.}
The fixed-target $f$-PG objective has an exact gradient formula (\cref{thm:m-fpg}). The implementation replaces the occupancies by plug-in estimates, removes the same-batch dependence by cross-fitting, and smooths both arguments, so that its sampled update is, conditionally on the fitted signal, an unbiased but noisy estimate of the gradient of a surrogate, not of the divergence (\cref{prop:m-fpg-approx}).
No theorem here says that the iteration converges, let alone to the expert.
In the finite mazes, where the signal is built from exact counts of simulated transitions, it nevertheless did so reliably: every run, goal and seed met the thresholds by the checkpoint at $\vn{s14.pct.lo}{40}$ to $\vn{s14.pct.hi}{60}\%$ of its budget (an upper bound at the $\vn{fpg.interval.f}{10{,}000}$-transition evaluation interval), with smooth curves and standard deviations across seeds of no more than about $\vn{s11.sd.fpg_max}{0.014}$ nats.
In the continuous maze the signal depends on a kernel density estimate and the policy on an MLP; the method progressed steadily but slowly and produced very stochastic policies that reach the goal without staying there.
The divergence was still falling when the budget ended.

\paragraph{FB imitation.}
The FB theory is exact only for exact representations, and \cref{prop:m-fb-rank} shows that a rank-16 model cannot be exact in the finite mazes, whose kernels have rank \vn{env.rank.4r}{40} or \vn{env.rank.open}{49}.
So the finite experiments test FB imitation outside the regime of its guarantees, and the oracle shows how far outside: over state--action pairs, the largest total-variation error of the model's successor measures is $\vn{s15.tv.lo}{36}$ to $\vn{s15.tv.hi}{80}$, for a true mass of $\vn{const.mass}{20}$.
The finite results are consistent with this picture.
\ERFB, which only reads a latent off the demonstrations, often reaches the goal but rarely stays.
\DMFB{} trusts the model further: it minimizes the model's estimate of the successor-measure distance, which it did successfully in all \vn{s11.dmpath.loss}{27} adaptations, and in \vn{abs.dm.js_up_finite}{16} of the \vn{abs.dm.n_finite}{18} finite ones the policy moved \emph{away} from the expert while doing so.
We read this as the latent optimizer exploiting the model's error, but the runs show the correlation, not the mechanism.
This is not a contradiction of FI24, whose experiments use much larger models and data; it shows that the matching step is only as good as the model it is computed with.
In the continuous maze, a nearly singular backward Gram matrix appeared in the pilot and again in every final model, and neither FB method imitated the expert.

\paragraph{Stability.}
Numerically, every method was stable: no failure, no non-finite value, no restart.
Statistically, the two families differ sharply.
$f$-PG's outcome hardly depends on the seed.
FB imitation depends on the seed and on the pretraining snapshot: the divergence of a run moved by up to $\vn{s11.swing}{0.53}$ nats between consecutive snapshots, and \vn{s15.third}{a third} of the finite curves reached a success of $0.80$ at some snapshot but lost it by the last.
A user of FB imitation would need a criterion to choose the snapshot, and none is available without evaluation rollouts or an oracle.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Three seeds} per configuration describe these runs; they do not support confidence intervals, significance tests or rankings beyond the observed counts.
\item \textbf{Small, simple environments.} Two $7\times7$ mazes and one continuous maze, with three goals each. Whether the conclusions transfer to larger state spaces, other dynamics or high-dimensional observations is untested.
\item \textbf{Narrow tuning.} The $f$-PG learning rates and the FB temperature were chosen on one seed and one goal; both learning rates are at the edge of their grid, the $\KL$ ablations reuse the $\JS$ rate, and the FB network sizes, update counts and \DMFB{} settings were not tuned at all. A better-tuned FB model could change the comparison.
\item \textbf{Fixed budgets.} The budgets are the ceilings of the specification. They were not chosen to be sufficient; continuous $f$-PG in particular was still improving.
\item \textbf{Evaluation choices.} The operational thresholds are study choices; success counts a single visit to the goal, so we also report staying in the goal and the return. The continuous divergence is a biased plug-in estimate on histogram cells, with a measured floor of $\vn{s12.floor.lo}{0.0005}$ to $\vn{s12.floor.hi}{0.0013}$ nats.
\item \textbf{State matching.} All objectives compare state distributions; none identifies the expert's actions, and the state--action divergences are diagnostics only.
\item \textbf{Scope of the theory.} The proofs cover population objectives and exact models. No statement here concerns the convergence of neural-network training or the quality of a trained model, and $f$-IRL was analysed but not run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What would test the explanations}

The runs suggest experiments that this study did not perform.
A finite FB model with $d\ge\vn{env.rank.open}{49}$, for which an exact representation exists, would show whether \DMFB{} improves on \ERFB{} once the model error is small, and whether the oracle's model error predicts the imitation error.
Tracking the true \DMFB{} objective along the latent path, which the oracle can compute in the finite mazes, would test the model-exploitation reading directly.
In the continuous maze, radial-basis inputs to $B$ as in FB21, a larger budget and a larger rank would test the candidate causes of the degenerate Gram matrix.
Longer continuous $f$-PG runs and a learning-rate grid that extends past its current edge would show where $f$-PG stops.
More seeds, and goals not used for tuning, would turn the descriptive counts into estimates.

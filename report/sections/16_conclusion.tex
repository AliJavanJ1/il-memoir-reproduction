\section{Conclusion: answers to the research questions}\label{sec:conclusion}

\begin{answerbox}{Q1. Mathematical principles, proofs, and exact versus approximate}
\textbf{Occupancy matching.} The discounted next-state occupancy determines the return of every state reward (\cref{prop:m-return}), so matching it imitates the expert as far as state rewards can tell, and no further: it does not determine the actions (\cref{ex:occ-blocked}).
\textbf{$f$-PG}, adapted to a fixed expert distribution, has the exact gradient of \cref{thm:m-fpg}: a policy gradient whose per-step signal is $g=f'(d/q)$ at the next state, with discount weights and baselines (\cref{cor:m-fpg-mc}). It is exact for exact occupancies. The algorithm replaces them by plug-in estimates, which bias the nonlinear signal; cross-fitting removes the additional same-batch dependence, so that the sampled update is, conditionally on the fitted signal, an unbiased but noisy estimate of the gradient of a surrogate with that signal held fixed, not of the divergence; and smoothing both arguments changes the objective (\cref{prop:m-fpg-approx}, \cref{tab:fpg-ledger}).
\textbf{$f$-IRL}'s reward gradient is a covariance with the weight $h_f=(f^\circ)'(1/u)$ (\cref{thm:m-firl}). It is exact under the hypothesis \eqref{eq:m-gibbs} that the learner's law over state sequences is the Gibbs law $\bar p(\tau)\exp(\sum_tr_\theta(s_t)/\alpha)/Z(\theta)$ with a base weight $\bar p$ that does not depend on $\theta$. An exact maximum-entropy policy is not shown to realize this law under stochastic dynamics \audit{U-7}, and a practical inner loop only approximates the policy; the learned reward is not unique in general.
\textbf{FB.} Successor measures satisfy a linear Bellman equation (\cref{prop:m-fb-bellman}); if a model $F^\top B\nu$ is exact for every latent and the policies are greedy, the single vector $z_r=\E_\nu[rB]$ optimizes any reward $r$ (\cref{thm:m-fb}). A rank-16 model cannot be exact in the finite mazes (\cref{prop:m-fb-rank}), softmax policies replace optimality by a temperature bound (\cref{prop:m-fb-softmax}), and only the conditional bound of \cref{thm:m-fb-approx} applies to trained models. If the transition kernel has a square-integrable density with respect to the replay measure, the pretraining loss is a Bellman residual up to a constant; its minibatch gradients are unbiased for the loss on the stored buffer, and there is no convergence guarantee (\cref{prop:m-fb-loss}).
\textbf{FB imitation.} \ERFB{} is reward maximization of $\dd\rho_e/\dd\nu$, not matching (\cref{prop:m-er}); the \DMFB{} loss expansion holds for exact models without greedy policies (\cref{thm:m-dm}), and the algorithm minimizes its model-based, truncated estimate (\cref{prop:m-dm-est}).
\end{answerbox}

\begin{answerbox}{Q2. Similarities, differences and use cases}
Both families imitate from expert states without rewards or actions, through discounted next-state objects that are linked by $d^+_\pi=(1-\gamma)\int\mu_0(\dd x)M^{\pi,+}(x,\cdot)$, and neither solves a Bellman optimality equation for the imitation task.
They are not the same objective: $f$-PG and $f$-IRL compare \emph{marginals}, \DMFB{} compares \emph{conditional} successor measures in a replay-weighted norm, and \ERFB{} compares nothing (\cref{tab:objectives}).
The main difference is where the approximation sits. $f$-PG samples the real dynamics and approximates the densities inside its signal; its sampled update is conditionally unbiased only for a surrogate gradient with the fitted signal held fixed. FB imitation does not interact after pretraining: \ERFB{} reads a latent off the demonstrations, and \DMFB{} takes a finite number of projected Adam steps on an empirical, truncated estimate of a model-based objective, computed with a model that cannot be exact in the finite mazes.
$f$-PG suits single tasks with a cheap simulator; $f$-IRL suits the case where a reward is wanted, although the reward it learns is not unique in general; FB imitation suits many tasks in one environment with expensive interaction, provided the model is accurate on the relevant states (\cref{sec:compare}).
\end{answerbox}

\begin{answerbox}{Q3. The role of the hyperparameters}
In $f$-PG and $f$-IRL the generator $f$ acts only through the shape of $v\mapsto f'(v)$ at the occupancy ratio: $\JS$ is bounded, $\KL(d\Vert q)$ is zero-forcing and needs smoothing, $\KL(q\Vert d)$ is mass-covering (\cref{sec:m-choice-f}). On one goal with three seeds, $\KL(d\Vert q)$ behaved like $\JS$, and $\KL(q\Vert d)$ first met the thresholds at the $\vn{s09.klqd.first.lo}{40{,}000}$- to $\vn{s09.klqd.first.hi}{50{,}000}$-transition checkpoints instead of $\vn{s09.js_kldq.first.lo}{20{,}000}$ to $\vn{s09.js_kldq.first.hi}{30{,}000}$ and kept more stochastic policies, at a learning rate tuned for $\JS$ (\cref{sec:ablations}).
The successor measure is learned by temporal differences on its measure-valued Bellman equation, with target networks, an orthonormality penalty and a latent distribution (\cref{sec:m-hyper-learn}). Its rank decides whether exactness is possible at all. For an exact model, the temperature and the latent norm act together as one inverse temperature (\cref{prop:m-fb-softmax}(d)); the trained forward network also depends on the latent, so for it this equivalence is not guaranteed. The orthonormality penalty pushes the Gram matrix of $B$ towards the identity, which encourages good conditioning and a fixed scale but does not guarantee them (\cref{prop:m-fb-orth}(c)): in the finite four-rooms ablation the models trained with it were well conditioned, and those trained without it were poorly conditioned and gave nearly uniform policies, an observation on three seeds that did not change whether the thresholds were met; in the continuous maze, with the penalty on, the Gram matrix was nearly singular ($\lambda_{\min}$ between $\vn{s12.lmin.lo}{1.7\times10^{-5}}$ and $\vn{s12.lmin.hi}{4.2\times10^{-5}}$). The learned model was not stationary at the end of pretraining: imitation quality moved by up to $\vn{s11.swing}{0.53}$ nats between snapshots.
\end{answerbox}

\begin{answerbox}{Q4. The finite scenario with exact planning}
With an exact oracle, three seeds and predeclared thresholds: all \vn{abs.fpg.n}{18} $f$-PG runs met the thresholds, first at the $\vn{abs.fpg.first.lo}{20{,}000}$- or $\vn{abs.fpg.first.hi}{30{,}000}$-transition checkpoint (evaluated every $\vn{fpg.interval.f}{10{,}000}$ transitions), and kept them; \ERFB{} met them at the end in \vn{abs.er.last}{2} of \vn{n.runs.finite}{18} runs and \DMFB{} in \vn{abs.dm.last}{none} (\cref{sec:results-finite}).
\DMFB{} lowered its model objective in every adaptation, yet moved away from the expert in \vn{abs.dm.js_up_finite}{16} of \vn{abs.dm.n_finite}{18}, while the FB model's measured errors made every FB guarantee vacuous.
All methods were numerically stable; FB imitation was statistically unstable across seeds and pretraining snapshots, $f$-PG was not.
For one task, both families used the same $\vn{budget.same.f}{50{,}000}$ transitions; FB amortizes to $\vn{s14.amortized.f}{16{,}667}$ per task over three goals but did not reach $f$-PG's quality, so no cost comparison at equal quality exists (\cref{sec:costs}).
\end{answerbox}

\begin{answerbox}{Q5. The continuous state space with a DQN-style approximation}
With a forward network that outputs one vector per action, FB pretraining on $\vn{replay.c}{100{,}000}$ task-blind transitions produced a backward representation with a nearly singular Gram matrix ($\lambda_{\min}$ between $\vn{s12.lmin.lo}{1.7\times10^{-5}}$ and $\vn{s12.lmin.hi}{4.2\times10^{-5}}$), and neither \ERFB{} nor \DMFB{} imitated the expert: both sometimes reach the goal, neither stays. $f$-PG with a KDE signal and an MLP policy ended closest (divergence $\vn{abs.cont.fpg.js.lo}{0.24}$ to $\vn{abs.cont.fpg.js.hi}{0.41}$ nats, success $\vn{abs.cont.fpg.succ.lo}{0.92}$ to $\vn{abs.cont.fpg.succ.hi}{0.99}$) but no method met the thresholds within its budget (\cref{sec:results-continuous}).
\end{answerbox}

\paragraph{In one sentence.}
In these small mazes, matching occupancies directly with policy gradients was reliable where the occupancies could be counted and made steady but incomplete progress in the continuous maze, while forward--backward imitation was limited by the accuracy of its learned successor measures, which neither its pretraining loss nor its matching objective could reveal.
